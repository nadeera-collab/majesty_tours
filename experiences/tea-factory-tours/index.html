What to expect

1

In the field: Where the leaf comes from

The visit starts in the rows. Two leaves and a bud is the standard pluck, and watching how quickly a practised picker works, and how little of the bush she takes, explains more about tea grades than any display board.

  • Walking the tea rows
  • Meeting the pickers
  • Two leaves and a bud

2

Withering: The first floor

Fresh leaf goes into long withering troughs where air is drawn through it for hours to take out the moisture and make it pliable enough to roll. The smell of this floor is the smell of the whole factory.

  • The withering troughs
  • Why the leaf has to lose moisture before it can be rolled
3

Rolling & oxidation: Where black tea becomes black

Rolling machines bruise and twist the withered leaf to break its cells open, and the oxidation that follows is what turns green leaf coppery brown and builds the flavour. It is timed to the minute and stopped deliberately.

  • The rolling machines
  • Oxidation beds and the colour change
  • How timing decides the character of the tea
4

Firing & grading: Drying and sorting

Hot air stops the oxidation dead and dries the tea down for storage. What comes out is then sorted by particle size through a series of meshes, which is all that tea grades really are.

  • The dryers
  • Grading meshes and sorting
  • BOP, BOPF, OP, fannings and dust
  • The grading room display
5

The tasting: The cupping table

The point of the visit. Several of the estate’s grades brewed to the same strength and timed the same way, tasted along the table so you can actually tell them apart, and understand what altitude does to a cup.

Best time to go

Any month works for the tour, but the factory is at its most interesting when it is actually running, which means a weekday, and ideally the afternoon or evening, when the day’s plucking is being processed. Processing pauses on Sundays and poya (full moon) days. For the fields themselves, the hill country is clearest from January to April.

Frequently asked questions

Will the factory actually be running?

On a weekday, usually yes, and it is worth planning for. Plucking happens in the morning and processing follows, so an afternoon visit is more likely to catch machinery in motion. Sundays and poya days are generally quiet.

Which factory will we visit?

It depends on where you are and where you are heading. There are working estates around Matale, Kandy and Nuwara Eliya, and we pick one that fits your route rather than detouring you for the sake of a name.

What is the difference between the grades?

Mostly particle size, not quality. After drying, the tea is sorted through meshes: whole-leaf grades like OP at the coarse end, then BOP, BOPF, fannings and dust. Smaller particles brew faster and stronger, which is why they end up in tea bags.

Does altitude really change the taste?

Noticeably, and the tasting is arranged so you can tell. High-grown tea from around Nuwara Eliya is lighter, brisker and more aromatic; mid-grown Kandy tea is fuller and maltier. Side by side at the same brew strength, the difference is obvious.

Can we buy tea at the factory?

Yes, and the estate shop is generally better value and fresher than the airport. Your guide will tell you what is worth buying and what is priced for visitors.

Is it suitable for children?

Yes. The machinery is genuinely interesting to watch and the walk through the fields is easy. The cupping table is the part they will be least patient with, so we keep that end brisk.
